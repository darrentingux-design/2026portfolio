Enterprise Product Design Bringing Positive Pay to Small Business Banking
Hero image Image unavailable
RoleUX Lead
TimelineQ4 2026
Scope180+ screens
Teams3 Agile squads (Dev Leads, BAs, QAs, Scrum Masters), plus accessibility and content partners
Positive pay is a cheque-fraud control. A business submits a file of the cheques it has issued. When a cheque is presented for payment, the bank cross-references it against that file. If the details do not match, the cheque is raised as an exception and the client decides what happens to it: Pay, Pay & Issue, or Return.
Goal Bring positive pay to the small-business platform. Clients had identified it as a significant missing capability. The service already existed on the corporate platform, so the work was not to invent it — it was to rebuild it for a different user on a modern platform.
Consideration
  1. The service existed on the corporate platform but could not be lifted across. The design language was years out of date and the user was different: on the small-business platform, positive pay would be run by owners, office managers, and bookkeepers — typically with two or three accounts entitled and no treasury function behind them.
  2. Delivery was split across three Agile squads — payments, user administration, and reporting — each owning a different piece of one continuous client journey. I was the only designer working across all three. This case study focuses on the payments squad: exceptions, issue files, voids, and managing issues and voids.
  3. Scope was not handed down. I defined it with the squads: what came across from the corporate platform, what was cut, and what was rebuilt.
  4. A fixed October release date, with no research budget and no user access. This was a parity-and-migration build. The evidence available was internal analytics, the corporate platform’s product owner, competitive analysis, and operations.
Outcome Positive pay shipped on schedule in October 2026, delivered across all three squads. The exception slideout pattern created for the service also went live, and is now the pilot for adoption across the platform’s other payment rails.

The Process Three squads delivering one service meant three places where it could quietly drift apart. I ran this same sequence — business scope through to QA — in all three, and repeating it was what kept the end-to-end experience coherent when the delivery structure wasn’t.
Current State Analysis
With access to the test environment and the live corporate product, I mapped its existing positive pay flows end to end — entitlement gates, approval states, validation rules, error paths, and edge cases. The map became the reference for conversations with developers and product owners about what to carry across, what to cut, and what to rebuild. Two changes came directly out of it: remove the exceptions summary, and change the approval structure for managing issues and voids.
Current state map Image unavailable
Competitor Analysis
I then reviewed how five other banks handle manage exceptions: Chase, Bank of Hawaii, California Bank Trust, FM Bank, and Vectra Bank.
Competitor analysis board Image unavailable
My own hypothesis for the exceptions table had been a left-to-right reading order — cheque details first, resolving into the decision at the far right. The review overturned it. Account number consistently came first, because filtering by account is how people approach the queue. Cheque number sat beside amount in every case, mirroring the layout of a physical cheque. I restructured the table around what I found rather than what I had reasoned my way to.
DESIGN DECISION 1 - The Slideout Problem
The constraint The three decisions on an exception — Pay, Pay & Issue, Return — were inherited from the corporate platform, where they were housed behind a decisions dropdown. The small-business platform has its own global pattern: click a row in a table and a slideout opens with the record’s details and the actions available on it. That pattern assumed roughly binary actions. An exception carries three mutually exclusive decisions alongside download details, modify, and delete, and positive pay was the first service to put real pressure on it.
The workshop I ran a workshop with two other designers. I set the context and the constraint, then each of us — myself included — had five minutes to sketch independently. We brought the sketches back, critiqued them together, and narrowed to three options.
Workshop board — exploration of slideout + solution options Image unavailable
What shipped
Utility and destructive actions — download details, modify, delete — collapse into an ellipsis menu on the left. The three decisions sit on the right, all presented at equal weight. Equal weight was deliberate. There is no safe default on a suspect cheque, and promoting one decision to primary would nudge clients toward paying or returning. The cutoff sharpens it: if nobody decides in time, the bank decides or the client’s configured default applies. Inaction already carries a consequence, so the interface has no business adding a thumb to the scale.

The option that shipped was a merge of all three designers’ sketches, and it shipped unchanged from the workshop.
Design Decision #2 - Payee Name Payee positive pay adds payee-name matching on top of cheque number and amount. Two things made it awkward. The corporate design displayed all four payee name slots at once even though the section is entirely optional. And whether the section is mandatory at all depends on the account selected — specifically, on whether that account has payee positive pay configured.
I proposed detecting this at account selection, so the form could resolve its own requirements the moment a client picked an account rather than presenting four fields of ambiguous status. It was ruled out on feasibility. This is the decision I would revisit first. The current design asks clients to reason about a requirement the system already knows the answer to.

Design Decision #3 - What not to build What not to build The exceptions summary was cut. On the corporate platform it aggregates exception activity across a treasury team’s portfolio of accounts. Clients on the small-business platform have two or three accounts entitled on average, so the volume that makes a summary useful does not exist. Nothing replaced it. This was a proportionality judgment rather than a validated finding, and it is worth being clear about which of the two it was. The approval structure for creating and managing issues and voids was also cut. Internal analytics showed only 1–2% of clients on the corporate platform had approvals configured, and the small-business segment was expected to be lower still. The stronger argument was operational: for the few who did configure it, an issue or void would sit in an approval queue — on a service where the work is time-sensitive and a queued item is a blocked one. Removing a control is a risk decision, and the product owners for both platforms aligned on it.
Barriers
An agile squad working against a waterfall team File import was delivered by a separate team running waterfall while my squad ran agile. The two flows run in parallel and have to converge: the file import summary page exits to issues and voids, and the manual summary page exits to manage issues and voids. If those exit points did not land in the same place, a client doing the same job by two routes would end up somewhere different. Managing import maps raised the same question — where it lives, and how a client navigates to it. I stayed close to the file import designers throughout, and we aligned on timelines and shared exit points. That extended to the DRS, where we had to confirm behaviour before the screens were technically dev-ready, because their delivery model needed the answer earlier than ours would have produced it.
Results And Impact
Positive pay shipped on schedule in October 2026 — 180+ screens across three Agile squads, delivered as one service. The exception slideout pattern went into production with it, extending a pattern library I govern on top of the bank’s enterprise design system. Positive pay is the pilot: if it holds up, the pattern rolls out to the platform’s other payment rails. The current state documentation outlived the project. It became the reference not only for my lead, but for services delivered after positive pay — reporting, stop payments, and cheque deposits.
Learnings
Documentation is the shared reference With three squads building one service and a fourth team delivering file import on a different methodology, the current state map was the only thing everyone could point at. It took a significant share of the project, and it was worth it — the same documentation went on to support services delivered after positive pay. Evidence beats reasoning I had a defensible argument for the exceptions table: information left to right, resolving into the decision. Reviewing five other banks showed me clients filter by account number first, and that cheque number sits beside amount because that is how a cheque is laid out. My reasoning was sound and still wrong. The best pattern came out of a room, not a file The slideout structure that shipped was a merge of three designers’ five-minute sketches. I set the constraint and facilitated the critique rather than drawing the answer, and the result was better than what I had going in.

At a glance During winter 2024 I had the pleasure of working with the School of Graduate Studies and lead the redesigning efforts of the website. The University of Toronto’s School of Graduate Studies (SGS) website serves as a pivotal platform to provide comprehensive information and resources at every phase and aspect of the graduate learning journey. This redesign project focuses on making improvements to the navigation design and most frequently sought-after user paths/flows, such as program applications for prospective students and writing support for current students and employees.

Problem Despite the university's reputation for excellence, its graduate studies website fails to meet users' needs due to design inconsistencies and content redundancy from multiple developer iterations. This results in poor information findability and inconsistent elements, significantly impacting the user experience for prospective students, current students, and staff. Addressing these issues is crucial to align with SGS's mission of providing comprehensive information and enhancing the graduate school's reputation.
Alignment Prior to starting our research it is crucial to understand first and foremost what are our client's objectives. We prioritized uncovering this during our first meeting with the clients.
Client Objective 1 To promote and support the work and supports offered by the SGS centres by ensuring their services are easy for students to find and they understand the benefits.
Client Objective 2 To support administrative staff and departments with the tools, messages and information they need to support their students.
Client Objective 3 To promote and support the communications needs and priorities of the Dean and Vice-Deans.
How might we improve the SGS website's navigation to ensure that it effectively meets its user groups' diverse needs by enhancing the findability of key application resources and educational support?
Research In our research, we identified three primary user groups for the SGS website: prospective students, current students, and staff. Through 13 interviews, we discovered that current students often bypass the website, relying on direct emails or faculty-specific sites. As a result, we focused our redesign on addressing the shared needs of staff and prospective students, while considering current students as secondary users.

Personas From our identified themes, we wanted to create two personas that embodied the frustrations of both students and staff at UofT. Incorporating insights from our interview, survey, and pain points, our personas allowed for a more focused and effective redesign of our ideal user.
As-Is Scenario Alongside our personas, we created As-Is scenarios capturing what our users are doing, feeling, thinking, and the pain points their experiencing throughout their user journey of the SGS Site. This highlighted for us what steps of the user flow are going wrong, which in turn increased our focus and efficiency on problem solving.
