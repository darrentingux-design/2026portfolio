# Case study: Positive Pay for small business banking (BMO)

Role: UX Lead. Timeline: Q4 2026. Scope: 180+ screens. Delivered across three Agile squads (payments, user administration, reporting) plus accessibility and content partners. Darren was the only designer working across all three squads. The case study focuses on the payments squad: exceptions, issue files, voids, and managing issues and voids.

What it is: Positive Pay is a cheque-fraud control. A business submits a file of the cheques it has issued. When a cheque is presented for payment, the bank checks it against that file. If the details don't match, the cheque is raised as an exception and the client decides what happens to it: Pay, Pay & Issue, or Return.

Goal: bring Positive Pay to the small-business platform, which clients had identified as a significant missing capability. The service already existed on BMO's corporate platform, so the job was to rebuild it for a different user on a modern platform, not to invent it.

Why it wasn't a copy-paste: the corporate design was years out of date, and the users were different. On the small-business platform, Positive Pay is run by owners, office managers, and bookkeepers, typically with two or three accounts and no treasury team behind them. There was a fixed October release date, no research budget, and no user access, so the evidence came from internal analytics, the corporate platform's product owner, competitive analysis, and operations. Scope wasn't handed down; Darren defined it with the squads: what came across, what was cut, and what was rebuilt.

Process: the same sequence from business scope through to QA in all three squads, which kept the end-to-end experience coherent. It began with a current state analysis: mapping the corporate product's flows end to end (entitlement gates, approval states, validation rules, error paths, edge cases), which became the reference for conversations with developers and product owners. A competitor review of five banks (Chase, Bank of Hawaii, California Bank Trust, FM Bank, and Vectra Bank) overturned Darren's own hypothesis for the exceptions table. He had expected a left-to-right reading order; the banks consistently led with account number, because people filter by account, and put cheque number beside amount, mirroring a physical cheque. He rebuilt the table around that.

Design decision 1, the slideout problem: the platform opens a slideout when you click a table row, a pattern built for roughly binary actions. An exception carries three mutually exclusive decisions (Pay, Pay & Issue, Return) alongside download, modify, and delete. Darren ran a workshop with two other designers: each sketched independently for five minutes, they critiqued together, and narrowed to three options. The shipped design is a merge of all three designers' sketches and shipped unchanged from the workshop. Utility and destructive actions collapse into an ellipsis menu on the left, and the three decisions sit on the right at equal weight. Equal weight was deliberate: there is no safe default on a suspect cheque, and if nobody decides in time the bank decides or the client's configured default applies, so the interface shouldn't push people toward paying or returning.

Design decision 2, payee name: payee positive pay adds payee-name matching on top of cheque number and amount. The corporate design showed all four payee-name fields at once even though the section is optional, and whether it is mandatory depends on the selected account. Darren proposed detecting that at account selection so the form could resolve its own requirements; it was ruled out on feasibility. He says this is the decision he would revisit first.

Design decision 3, what not to build: Darren cut the exceptions summary, which on the corporate platform aggregates activity across a treasury team's accounts. Small-business clients have too few accounts for a summary to be useful, and he is clear that this was a proportionality judgment, not a validated finding. The approval structure for creating and managing issues and voids was also cut, with the product owners for both platforms aligned on the decision.

Barriers: a separate team delivered file import on a waterfall model while Darren's squad ran Agile. The two flows run in parallel and have to converge, so he stayed close to the file import designers and aligned on timelines and shared exit points, including confirming behaviour before screens were technically dev-ready.

Outcome: Positive Pay shipped on schedule in October 2026, with 180+ screens across three squads delivered as one service. The exception slideout pattern also went live and is now the pilot for adoption across the platform's other payment rails. The current-state documentation outlived the project and became the reference for later services such as reporting, stop payments, and cheque deposits.

Learnings: documentation is the shared reference when several teams build one service; evidence beats reasoning (the competitor review showed that his well-reasoned hypothesis was wrong); and the best pattern came out of a room, not a file, since he set the constraint and facilitated rather than drawing the answer himself.

# Case study: University of Toronto School of Graduate Studies (SGS) website

Role: Project Manager and UX Designer. Timeline: Winter 2024 (4 months). Tools: Figma, Canva, Miro. Team: Erica Lee (UX research), Valerie Wu (UX design), Colin Xiao (UX design and client contact), Lu Liu (UX design), and Darren. SGS was the client, and the project ran over four months.

The SGS website serves prospective students, current students, and staff, but years of developer iterations left it with inconsistent design and redundant content, so information was hard to find. The team redesigned the navigation and the most-sought-after paths, such as program applications for prospective students and writing support for current students and staff.

Process: research (a questionnaire, moderated interviews, and observation tasks across 13 participants: 4 prospective students, 4 staff, and 5 current students), then an affinity diagram and a feasibility-versus-impact prioritization grid to cope with the four-month timeline. That surfaced five pain points: poor information hierarchy, no help when scrolling long content, sections that are hard to tell apart, information overload, and links and buttons that lack context. The team built personas and as-is scenarios, brainstormed "no-brainer" solutions, then moved through mid-fidelity and high-fidelity prototypes and storyboards, and finished with usability testing. Interviews showed current students often skip the website, so the redesign focused on prospective students and staff, with current students as secondary users.

Impact: average user satisfaction rose from 6.12 to 9.05 out of 10, average search time for key resources dropped by 86%, and content redundancy across crucial workflows was eliminated. Accessibility work included WCAG AAA colour contrast, generous line and paragraph spacing, descriptive links, and proper headings for screen readers.

Takeaways: efficient project management, design artifacts as a communication tool with clients, and taking time to understand what the client wants. Next steps would be a second round of usability testing and extending the changes to other parts of the site.

# Case study: Acer Health Management Center

Role: Lead UX Designer, and the sole UX designer on the project. Timeline: Summer 2023. Tools: Figma and Attention Insight. Team: Eason Xing (PM) and Gene Wang (software engineer). Darren was an intern at Acer, responsible for the design direction of the proof of concept.

The project was a collaboration between Acer and National Taiwan University Hospital (NTUH) to digitize healthcare services in Taiwan by making personalized risk predictions accessible, while streamlining how clinicians analyze incoming data. The deliverables were a B2C mobile app that lets people run health risk predictions based on NTUH's risk prediction model, and a B2B web application that takes data from the app and shows it to NTUH doctors for analysis and diagnosis.

Process: conceptualization, user flows, information architecture, wireframes and prototypes, iteration, AI-assisted testing, and a final proof-of-concept design. Because it is often hard to get PMs and executives to allocate time for usability testing, Darren used an AI attention-prediction tool to see where users' eyes would go and iterated on that. One analysis showed an optimal clarity of 58%, which is below average.

Impact: higher stakeholder satisfaction that secured further development of the project, more readable and accessible key patient information for doctors and nurses, and a refined patient log-in flow that reduced confusion and enabled smooth health-data integration.

Takeaways: coming from an academic background, Darren learned to balance feasibility with aesthetics by working with developers and business stakeholders; he gained experience collaborating across disciplines; and he learned that designing a mobile and web ecosystem means understanding who the users are and how data is shared and visualized between the two.
